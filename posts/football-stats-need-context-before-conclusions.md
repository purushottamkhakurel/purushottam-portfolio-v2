# Football stats need context: what the numbers don't tell you

At the final whistle, the score tells us what happened. The statistics help us explore how it happened.

That is useful, but it is also where football conversations can become misleading. One person points to possession. Another shares expected goals. Someone else counts shots or pass accuracy. Each number becomes proof that their interpretation of the match is correct.

The problem is not the statistics. It is asking one statistic to explain an entire game.

Football is too fluid for that. A number can give us a useful clue, but we still need to understand the question behind it and the situation in which it was recorded.

## Start with the question, not the statistic

Before looking at a match dashboard, decide what you want to understand.

Are you asking which team created better chances? Whether a side moved the ball into dangerous areas? How effectively it pressed? Or whether an individual player performed their role well?

Those are different questions. They need different evidence.

For example, shots can tell us how often a team attempted to score. They do not tell us whether those attempts were clear chances. Pass completion tells us how many attempted passes reached a teammate. It does not tell us whether those passes helped the team progress.

A useful analysis begins with a question and then chooses the metric that can help answer it.

## Expected goals measure chance quality, not the final verdict

Expected goals, usually written as xG, estimates how likely a shot is to become a goal based on similar attempts in the past. The model can consider factors such as distance, angle, shot type, defensive pressure and the goalkeeper's position.

A shot valued at 0.1 xG represents a chance that similar attempts have been scored roughly one time in ten. Add the values of a team's shots together and you get a picture of the chances it created.

That is helpful because two teams can take the same number of shots but create very different opportunities.

However, a higher xG total does not prove that a team should have won. It describes the quality of the shots, not every part of the match. A red card, a goalkeeper's performance, a defensive mistake or the timing of a goal can change how a game unfolds. In a single match, a small number of chances can also produce an unexpected scoreline.

Use xG to ask whether a team created good opportunities. Do not use it to erase the actual result or to claim that one metric has settled the debate.

It is also worth remembering that xG is a model, not a universal measurement with one fixed formula. When comparing figures, check which provider produced them and avoid treating every model as identical.

## Possession is not automatically control

A team can have most of the ball without creating much danger.

It might circulate passes between its centre-backs and midfielders while the opponent stays compact and protects the middle. Another team might have less possession but use each recovery to attack quickly into open space.

Neither approach is automatically better. The important question is what the possession achieved.

Look at where the ball was carried or passed, how often a team reached the final third, whether it entered the penalty area and whether its attacks created shots from useful positions. The number of passes alone cannot explain those details.

Possession can be part of control, but control also involves territory, pressure, defensive organisation and the ability to manage dangerous moments.

## Pass accuracy needs a purpose

A player completing 92% of passes may have had an excellent game. They may also have played mostly short, low-risk passes.

Another player might complete fewer passes because they repeatedly try to break defensive lines or deliver the ball into difficult areas. That does not automatically make the second player better, either. Risky passes can be wasteful when the situation does not call for them.

The context matters: the player's position, instructions, teammates' movement and the type of passes attempted.

Opta, for example, defines a progressive pass as a completed pass that moves the ball significantly closer to the opponent's goal. Looking at progression alongside pass completion can tell us more than either number on its own.

When judging a midfielder, ask whether their passing helped the team escape pressure, move forward, create space or reach attackers. A percentage cannot answer all of that by itself.

## Defensive numbers need context too

A defender with many clearances or tackles may have performed important work. But a high count can also reflect how much time their team spent under pressure.

A side that dominates possession may not need to make as many defensive actions. A side that defends deep may record more blocks and clearances simply because it faces more attacks.

Instead of treating a high total as proof of a good or bad performance, look at what the team allowed. Did it protect central areas? Did it force opponents into less dangerous positions? Could it regain the ball and move forward, or did it keep defending the same attacks?

For individual players, consider their role and the defensive structure around them. A centre-back, full-back and defensive midfielder are asked to solve different problems. Comparing their raw totals without that context can be unfair.

## Five questions to ask after a match

A simple routine can make football statistics much more useful:

1. **What am I trying to understand?** Chance creation, progression, pressing, defending or an individual contribution?
2. **What does this statistic actually measure?** Read the definition instead of relying on the label.
3. **What was happening in the match?** Consider the score, opponent, player roles, red cards and tactical changes.
4. **Does the match footage support the interpretation?** Look for the actions behind the number.
5. **Is this a one-match observation or a longer pattern?** A run of performances usually gives a more reliable picture than one evening.

You do not need a complicated dashboard to follow this approach. Even two or three well-chosen metrics, combined with watching the game, can lead to a better discussion.

## Let the numbers improve the conversation

Statistics should not replace watching football. They should help us notice things we might otherwise miss and give us better questions to ask.

A team can lose while creating better chances. A player can complete fewer passes while taking more responsibility to move the ball forward. A defender can make fewer tackles because the team is preventing attacks earlier.

None of those explanations should be accepted automatically. They are starting points for looking closer.

The best use of football data is not to win an argument with a screenshot. It is to understand the game a little better.
