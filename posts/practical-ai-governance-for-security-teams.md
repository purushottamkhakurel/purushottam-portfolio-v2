# A practical place to start with AI governance

AI tools are finding their way into ordinary work. People use them to summarize documents, draft emails, write code, analyze information and help with decisions. Some of these uses are already part of everyday routines, sometimes without much discussion about the risks.

For security and GRC teams, that creates a fairly simple starting question: do we know where AI is being used, and what information is being shared with it?

## Find out which tools people are using

It is difficult to manage something the organization cannot see. Start by making a list of the AI services people use, including features built into software the company already pays for.

For each use, it helps to know who owns it, why it is being used, what data goes into it and whether its output affects an important decision. The list does not need to be complicated. It just needs to give the organization a clearer picture.

## Pay attention to the information being shared

Not every AI tool is suitable for every kind of information. Customer records, personal information, passwords, confidential documents and internal business data all need careful handling.

People should know which tools are approved and what they can safely share. A policy is useful, but practical examples and clear guidance are much easier for employees to follow.

## Do not let responsibility disappear

AI can produce a response that sounds convincing and still be wrong. It can miss context, make up details or provide an answer that needs checking.

For work that matters, someone should review the output before it is used. It should also be clear who is accountable for the final decision. AI can help with the work, but it should not become an excuse for nobody taking responsibility.

## Remember the vendors behind the tools

When an organization uses an external AI service, it should understand how that provider handles information. Questions about data retention, model training, access controls, subprocessors and changes to the service are worth asking.

The answers may differ from one provider to another, so it is important to look at the actual service and its terms rather than make assumptions.

## Keep reviewing as things change

AI tools and the way people use them can change quickly. A review that made sense six months ago may no longer describe the current use.

A practical programme can begin with an inventory, clear rules for data, named owners, human review for important outputs and regular checks of vendors and use cases.

AI governance does not have to mean stopping people from trying useful tools. It means understanding how they are being used and making sure the organization has sensible safeguards in place.
